# AI-Assisted Motif Mapping of Scotland’s Carved Stone Balls: The CSB-AI Methodology

Scotland’s Carved Stone Balls (CSBs) represent one of the most intriguing and enigmatic classes of objects from the Neolithic period. With hundreds of these intricately carved stone objects found mostly in Aberdeenshire, their exact purpose remains a mystery. While archaeologists have studied their shapes and patterns for decades, recent advances in artificial intelligence offer a new lens through which to examine these artifacts.

In this article, I'll walk you through the **CSB-AI** project, a methodology that repurposes a modern computer vision pipeline to surface visual similarities across these ancient carvings. The goal isn't to assign meaning or date to the objects, but rather to provide archaeologists and enthusiasts with a new interactive "motif map" to explore families of similar patterns.

---

## The Core Concept: Finding Families of Carvings

At the heart of CSB-AI is the idea of treating the carved surfaces as a collection of "motifs." By breaking down high-resolution photographs of the balls into smaller patches, we can analyze the specific carvings—spirals, concentric circles, hatching, and more—independently of the ball's overall shape.

The methodology is built around a five-step pipeline:

1. **Patch Extraction**
2. **Visual Embeddings (CLIP & HOG)**
3. **Dimensionality Reduction (PCA + UMAP)**
4. **Clustering**
5. **Interactive Exploration**

Let's dive into each step.

---

## 1. Patch Extraction

To analyze the intricate details of the carvings, we first divide each photograph of a Carved Stone Ball into a grid of small squares. This process isolates individual carved elements—what we call "patches."

By focusing only on the carved regions, we discard the smooth or uncarved surfaces, ensuring that the subsequent analysis focuses purely on the craftsmanship and motifs. Each of these tiny patches becomes a single data point in our final map.

## 2. Visual Embeddings: Giving Patches a "Fingerprint"

Once we have our patches, we need a way for a computer to "see" and compare them. We achieve this by converting each image patch into a mathematical representation known as an **embedding** or "visual fingerprint."

We use two different approaches to generate these fingerprints:

*   **CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining):** This deep neural model, developed by OpenAI, is excellent at semantic grouping. It understands complex visual features and helps group patches that share similar structural motifs, even if the lighting or exact texture varies.
*   **HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradients):** As a lightweight, traditional computer vision baseline, HOG focuses purely on texture and edges. It provides a robust comparison point against the deep learning approach.

## 3. Dimensionality Reduction: PCA + UMAP

The visual embeddings generated by models like CLIP are highly complex, existing in hundreds of dimensions. To visualize this data on a 2D screen, we need to reduce its dimensionality while preserving the relationships between the patches.

We employ a two-stage approach:
1.  **PCA (Principal Component Analysis):** First, we use PCA to reduce the initial high dimensionality to a more manageable number, retaining the most significant variations in the data.
2.  **UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection):** Next, we apply UMAP. UMAP is exceptional at preserving local data structures, meaning patches that are mathematically similar (i.e., visually similar) in high-dimensional space remain close together when mapped to 2D.

The resulting coordinates form the basis of our scatter plot. Even though the X and Y axes don't represent physical measurements like centimeters, distance on the map represents visual similarity.

## 4. Clustering: Highlighting Motif Families

With our patches plotted in 2D space, we apply clustering algorithms (like K-means) to mathematically group the points into "families."

These clusters represent sets of look-alike carvings. We assign a unique color to each cluster, allowing users to immediately identify broad categories of motifs across the entire dataset. For example, one color might represent tight spirals, while another represents cross-hatching.

## 5. Interactive Explorer: Bringing it to the Web

The final piece of the puzzle is making this data accessible to non-technical users, particularly archaeologists and historians. We built a responsive, interactive frontend using modern web technologies:

*   **React:** Powers the user interface, managing state and component logic.
*   **Plotly.js (via react-plotly.js):** Handles the rendering of the complex 2D scatter plot using WebGL for high performance, even with thousands of data points.

The interface allows users to:
*   View the "motif map" where every dot is a tiny patch of carving.
*   Toggle specific cluster families on and off.
*   Filter the map to show patches from specific Carved Stone Balls.
*   **Compare feature:** Click on any two dots to view the high-resolution patches side-by-side, complete with metadata about the specific ball and motif family.

We also included an "Admin Mode" that allows researchers to select pairs of interesting patches and export them as structured "Case Studies" (JSON format) for further review.

---

## Conclusion

The CSB-AI project demonstrates how standard computer vision techniques—typically used for classifying modern images or searching databases—can be creatively applied to archaeological artifacts. By transforming carved surfaces into a navigable "motif map," we offer a novel way to visualize connections across Scotland's Carved Stone Balls.

The tool doesn't provide answers about *why* these objects were made, but it provides a powerful new lens for exploring the *how* and the *what*, potentially surfacing visual relationships that might be missed by the naked eye.
